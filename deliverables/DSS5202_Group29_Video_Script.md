## Slide 1. Project purpose

0:00–0:44. Vanshika. 86 spoken words.

Hello, we are Group 29: Aditya, Apurwa and Vanshika. Our project studies how Singapore can meet the additional cooling-water demand from new data centres. We compare increasing water supply with reducing the amount of water required at each site. The analysis covers growth between 2027 and 2035 and considers the decisions facing PUB, IMDA and data-centre operators. Our main finding is a trade-off. Lower water use can require more electricity, so the preferred option depends on the cooling design and the priorities used to assess it.

## Slide 2. Problem and study boundary

0:44–1:43. Vanshika. 116 spoken words.

Singapore provisionally awarded 200 megawatts of data-centre capacity under the second call for applications in 2026. Our starting question is how much additional cooling water this growth could require. We use three capacity scenarios, with 200, 500 and 700 megawatts by 2035. The larger cases are planning assumptions, not forecasts of approved projects. We estimate demand from capacity, utilisation and water-use effectiveness. Water-use effectiveness is the volume of water used for each unit of IT energy. We then compare the water-production and cooling changes needed to serve the same amount of IT activity. The functional unit is heat rejection for one megawatt-hour of IT energy. This keeps the supply and efficiency options on a comparable basis.

## Slide 3. Four strategies and a common baseline

1:43–2:57. Aditya. 145 spoken words.

The four strategies differ in water supply and heat rejection. A1 uses wet cooling with desalinated water. A2 uses wet cooling with NEWater. A3 also uses NEWater, but operates the cooling tower at higher cycles of concentration and recovers part of its blowdown. A4 uses dry heat rejection with wet assistance and NEWater for the remaining demand. All four share the same liquid-cooled IT baseline. We therefore count only differences attributable to the water and heat-rejection strategy. In particular, A4 receives no separate energy-saving credit simply for adopting liquid cooling. We include its additional fan and pump electricity, as well as incremental equipment cost. This boundary is essential to interpreting the results. Blowdown is the water discharged to control dissolved solids in a cooling tower. A3 assumes 8 cycles of concentration and recovers 70% of that discharge. These are operating assumptions that require water-quality checks.

## Slide 4. Water demand

2:57–4:02. Aditya. 129 spoken words.

For the 200-megawatt scenario, conventional wet cooling needs approximately 2.58 million cubic metres of water in 2035 under the assumed utilisation. The corresponding figure in the 700-megawatt scenario is 9.01 million cubic metres. Tower reuse reduces water demand by about 30% relative to A2. Dry rejection reduces it by about 76%, leaving 0.61 million cubic metres in the 200-megawatt case. These values follow a heat balance and the selected operating assumptions. Historical hourly weather provides a screening estimate for wet assistance. It does not establish that a particular dry cooler can meet every hour of cooling demand. Equipment design and site measurements are still needed. The mature utilisation assumption is 70%. The demand values therefore do not represent continuous operation at the full rated IT load.

## Slide 5. Greenhouse-gas results

4:02–4:57. Apurwa. 108 spoken words.

The greenhouse-gas comparison includes water production, delivery and the incremental electricity used for heat rejection. Common IT and liquid-loop burdens sit outside this partial boundary. The base results are 3.42 kilograms of carbon dioxide equivalent per megawatt-hour of IT for A1, 0.99 for A2, 0.74 for A3 and 19.71 for A4. Tower reuse has the lowest emissions within this model boundary. A4 saves water, but its additional electricity can outweigh the avoided emissions from water production. The grid-emission scenarios test how that balance changes. We interpret the results as a comparison of these strategies, rather than a complete carbon footprint of a data centre.

## Slide 6. Cost results

4:57–6:07. Aditya. 136 spoken words.

We separate the resource cost to society from the bill faced by an operator. The social calculation uses water-production and cooling costs, excluding tariffs that transfer money between organisations. The operator calculation uses the water tariff and commercial financing assumptions. Base social costs are 2.60 Singapore dollars per megawatt-hour for A1, 1.00 for A2, 1.54 for A3 and 11.60 for A4. NEWater is the least costly base option. This does not mean it minimises water demand. The cost and water results answer different questions. We report carbon monetisation separately as a sensitivity case, so an assumed carbon price does not silently determine the main financial comparison. A3 costs the operator 0.42 dollars less than A2 per megawatt-hour in the base case, because its lower water bill offsets the added recovery equipment.

## Slide 7. Assessment of broader priorities

6:07–7:06. Apurwa. 117 spoken words.

The Analytic Hierarchy Process combines the calculated results with broader concerns, including water security and implementation. The balanced scenario ranks the strategies in the order A3, A2, A1, A4. These weights are illustrative. They are informed by published concerns, but they are not measured preferences of PUB or any operator. We also test different weighting scenarios, changes in subcriterion weights and the effect of reducing overlap between criteria. A low consistency ratio checks the internal consistency of a comparison matrix. It does not validate the evidence or make a judgement objective. The AHP results therefore support discussion of the trade-offs. They should be read alongside the original water, carbon and cost values shown in the preceding slides.

## Slide 8. Sensitivity and uncertainty

7:06–8:14. Vanshika. 133 spoken words.

The sensitivity analysis asks which assumptions could change the decision. The chart varies the additional PUE associated with dry rejection while holding its capital cost at the base assumption. Each line shows A4's social cost minus the comparator's cost. A value above zero means A4 is more expensive. We also vary capital costs and model parameters, then test uncertainty in preferences separately. In the combined parameter and preference experiment, A3 ranks first in 76% of draws under the full hierarchy. This frequency is conditional on the ranges and distributions we selected. It is not a forecast probability that a technology will be best at a future site. Negative PUE values on this chart represent hypothetical electricity savings. They help locate the break-even condition, while the main uncertainty range assumes a positive dry-rejection penalty.

## Slide 9. Recommendations and validation needs

8:14–9:10. Apurwa. 110 spoken words.

For PUB, the results support assessing water savings together with the energy needed to achieve them. NEWater is a relevant supply benchmark, while tower reuse offers a further reduction in demand that should be checked against treatment cost and water quality. For IMDA and operators, the main requirement is measured performance. A cooling proposal should report both water-use and power-use effectiveness under expected loads and hot-weather conditions. Dry rejection deserves consideration where water reduction is a priority, but its electricity penalty and equipment cost need a site-specific assessment. The public evidence available here supports a screening comparison. It does not support imposing one universal technology choice on every new facility.

## Slide 10. Conclusion

9:10–10:00. Vanshika. 98 spoken words.

Our project connects data-centre growth to a water-planning decision. Supplying water through NEWater or desalination and reducing demand through cooling changes have different consequences. Dry rejection uses the least water in the base case, while NEWater has the lowest social cost. The comparison depends on the system boundary, the electricity penalty and the priorities used in the assessment. We provide the model and its assumptions so that these can be checked and updated when site data become available. The practical recommendation is to evaluate water and electricity together and keep the conditions behind each result visible. Thank you.
